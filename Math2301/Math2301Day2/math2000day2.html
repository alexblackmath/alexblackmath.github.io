    Span’s Labyrinth

    🔗

    Section 1 Vector Spaces

    Vectors had two primary operations that we could use:
    🔗
    • Addition: \(\vec{u} + \vec{v}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • Scalar Multiplication: \(\lambda \vec{u}\text{,}\) where \(\lambda \in \mathbb{R}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    The book gives a more detailed list of definitions to say what constitutes addition and scalar multiplication and enforces that they play well together. It even notes that it doesn’t have to use the real numbers \(\mathbb{R}\text{.}\) Instead, one could use a more general set of numbers. This can be very useful for modeling certain problems.
    🔗
    There are a few axioms I do want to highlight, because they are less obvious and yield good reminders of proof writing. One of these is that a vector space \(V\) has to have a notion of a zero vector typically denoted \(\vec{0}\text{.}\) This vector has the remarkable defining property that for any other vector \(\vec{u} \in V\text{,}\) we have
    \begin{equation*} \vec{0} + \vec{u} = \vec{u}. \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Theorem 1.1.

    Any vector space \(V\) has a unique zero vector \(\vec{0} \in V\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    That it has some zero vector comes for free by definition. But there could be two or three or infinitely many for all we know. We will do this by proof by contradiction.
    🔗
    Suppose for the sake of contradiction that there were two zero vectors \(\vec{0}\) and \(\vec{0}'\text{.}\) In particular, that means \(\vec{0} \neq \vec{0}'\text{.}\) However, by definition of a zero vector,
    \begin{equation*} \vec{0} = \vec{0} + \vec{0}' = \vec{0}' + \vec{0} = \vec{0}'. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    This is an absolute classic proof. If we didn’t talk about the axioms, we’d miss it. Another less obvious axiom is that vectors need to have additive inverses. That is, for all \(\vec{v} \in V\text{,}\) there exists \(-\vec{v} \in V\) such that \(\vec{v} + -\vec{v} = \vec{0}\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Theorem 1.2.

    Let \(V\) be a vector space. Let \(\vec{u} \in V\text{.}\) Then \((-1)\vec{u}\) is the unique additive inverse of \(\vec{u}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    Note that
    \begin{equation*} \vec{u} + (-1)\vec{u} = (1)\vec{u} + (-1)\vec{u} = (1+-1)\vec{u} = 0\vec{u} = \vec{0}. \end{equation*}
    Thus, \((-1) \vec{u}\) is an additive inverse of \(\vec{u}\text{.}\) Let \(-\vec{u}\) be any other additive inverse.
    \begin{equation*} -\vec{u} = -\vec{u} + \vec{0} = -\vec{u} + (\vec{u} + (-1)\vec{u}) = (-\vec{u} + \vec{u}) + (-1)\vec{u} = \vec{0} + (-1)\vec{u} = (-1)\vec{u}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    The book has some fun facts like those that you can prove alongside a detailed discussion of the axioms. In all of the examples we care about in this class with my way of teaching it, how to scale and add will be apparent and the properties familiar.
    🔗
    Examples of vector spaces include:
    🔗
    • \(\mathbb{R}^{n}\text{,}\) the set of \(n\)-tuples of real numbers
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(\displaystyle \mathbb{C}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • Matrices under addition and rescaling every entry
      🔗
      🔗
    • Polynomials
      🔗
      🔗
    • Functions from \(\mathbb{R}\) to \(\mathbb{R}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • Continuous functions from \(\mathbb{R}\) to \(\mathbb{R}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • Differentiable functions from \(\mathbb{R}\) to \(\mathbb{R}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • Solutions to linear ordinary differential equations
      🔗
      🔗
    I usually think of vectors as lists of numbers, but on a network with either the nodes or the connection between nodes inheriting weights. This leads to all kinds of applications of linear algebra.
    🔗
    🔗

    Section 2 Linear Subspaces

    A lot of examples we care about will come from subsets of vector spaces that are themselves vector spaces. There is a nice general condition for when that occurs:
    🔗

    Theorem 2.1.

    Let \(V\) be a vector space. Let \(U \subseteq V\text{,}\) and suppose that
    🔗
    • \(\displaystyle \vec{0} \in U\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • Closure Under Addition: For all \(\vec{u}, \vec{v} \in U\text{,}\) \(\vec{u} + \vec{v} \in U\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • Closure Under Scalar Multiplication: For \(\vec{u} \in U\) and \(\lambda \in \mathbb{R}\text{,}\) \(\lambda \vec{u} \in U\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    Then \(U\) is a vector space with the same notion of scalar multiplication.
    🔗
    🔗
    Such a subset is called a linear subspace. Axler proves this from the axioms, because he’s a hardcore guy. You can read that if you want, but it’s not the kind of thing I want to prioritize in this class.
    🔗
    For a set of vectors \(S \subseteq V\text{,}\) one can construct \(\text{span}(S)\text{,}\) the set of all vectors one can obtain by adding and rescaling finitely many of those vectors. For example,
    🔗
    • \(\displaystyle \text{span}(\vec{0}) = \{\vec{0}\}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(\text{span}((1,1)) = \{(x,x): x \in \mathbb{R}\}\text{,}\) which is the line \(y = x\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(\text{span}((1,0,0), (0,1,0)) = \{(x,y,0): x, y \in \mathbb{R}\}\) is the \(xy\)-plane in \(\mathbb{R}^{3}\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗

    Theorem 2.2.

    Let \(V\) be a vector space. For any nonempty subset \(S \subseteq V\text{,}\) \(\text{span}(S)\) is a linear subspace of \(V\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    First, since \(S \neq \emptyset\text{,}\) there exists an element \(\vec{w} \in S\text{.}\) Then \(\vec{0} = 0 \vec{w} \in \text{span}(S)\text{,}\) so \(\text{span}(S)\) contains the zero vector of \(V\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Let \(\vec{u}, \vec{v} \in \text{span}(S)\) and \(\lambda \in \mathbb{R}\text{.}\) Then, by definition, there exist \(\vec{s}_{1}, \dots, \vec{s}_{m}, \vec{t}_{1}, \dots, \vec{t}_{n} \in S\) and \(a_{1}, \dots, a_{m}, b_{1}, \dots, b_{n} \in \mathbb{R}\) such that
    \begin{equation*} \vec{u} = \sum_{i=1}^{m} a_{i} \vec{s}_{i} \text{ and } \vec{v} = \sum_{j=1}^{n} b_{i} \vec{t}_{j}. \end{equation*}
    Then
    \begin{equation*} \vec{u} + \vec{v} = \sum_{i=1}^{m} a_{i} \vec{s}_{i} + \sum_{j=1}^{n} b_{i} \vec{t}_{j}, \end{equation*}
    so \(\vec{u} + \vec{v}\) is a linear combination of elements of \(S\) and therefore in \(\text{span}(S)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Furthermore,
    \begin{equation*} \lambda \vec{u} = \lambda \left( \sum_{i=1}^{m} a_{i} \vec{s}_{i}\right) = \sum_{i=1}^{m} \lambda a_{i} \vec{s}_{i}, \end{equation*}
    which is a linear combination of elements of \(S\) and therefore in \(\text{span}(S)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Therefore, \(\text{span}(S)\) contains \(\vec{0}\) and is closed under addition and scalar multiplication and is thus a linear subspace.
    🔗
    🔗
    It turns out every single linear subspace arises in this way.
    🔗

    Lemma 2.3.

    Let \(V\) be a vector space. Then any finite linear combination of elements of \(V\) is in \(V\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    To prove this, we proceed by induction. Let \(\vec{u} \in V\text{,}\) and suppose that \(\vec{u}\) is a linear combination of \(1\) element of \(V\text{.}\) Then \(\vec{u} = \lambda \vec{v}\) for some \(\vec{v} \in V\) and \(\lambda \in \mathbb{R}\text{.}\) Since \(V\) is closed under scaling and \(\vec{v} \in V\) \(\lambda \vec{v} \in V\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Suppose for the sake of induction that any linear combination of \(m\) elements of \(V\) is in \(V\text{.}\) We want to show that any linear combination of \(m+1\) elements of \(V\) must be in \(V\text{.}\) Let
    \begin{equation*} \vec{u} = \sum_{i=1}^{m+1} a_{i} \vec{v}_{i} \end{equation*}
    be such a linear combination so that \(a_{1}, \dots, a_{m+1} \in \mathbb{R}\) and \(\vec{v}_{1}, \dots, \vec{v}_{m+1} \in V\text{.}\) Let
    \begin{equation*} \vec{w} = \sum_{i=1}^{m} a_{i} \vec{v}_{i}. \end{equation*}
    Then \(\vec{w}\) is a linear combination of \(m\) elements of \(V\text{,}\) so by our inductive hypothesis, \(\vec{w} \in V\text{.}\) Furthermore, since \(V\) is closed under scaling \(a_{m+1}\vec{v}_{m+1} \in V\text{.}\) Then, since \(V\) is closed under addition
    \begin{equation*} \vec{u} = \sum_{i=1}^{m+1} a_{i} \vec{v}_{i} = \sum_{i=1}^{m} a_{i} \vec{v}_{i} + a_{m+1} \vec{v}_{m+1} = \vec{u} + a_{m+1} \vec{v}_{m+1} \in V. \end{equation*}
    Therefore, any finite linear combination of elements of \(V\) is in \(V\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Theorem 2.4.

    Let \(V\) be a vector space, and let \(U \subseteq V\) be a subspace. Then
    \begin{equation*} \text{span}(U) = U. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    How do we prove two sets are equal? We show both inclusions.
    🔗
    Thus, we first show \(\text{span}(U) \supseteq U\text{.}\) Let \(\vec{u} \in U\text{.}\) Then \(\vec{u} = 1\vec{u}\text{,}\) which is a linear combination of some set of elements of \(U\text{.}\) Hence, \(\vec{u} \in \text{span}(U)\text{.}\) Therefore \(\text{span}(u) \supseteq U\text{.}\)
    🔗
    For the reverse direction, we show \(\text{span}(U) \subseteq U\text{.}\) Let \(\vec{u} \in \text{span}(U)\text{.}\) Then, by the definition of span, \(\vec{u}\) is a finite lienar combination of elements of \(U\text{.}\) By Lemma 2.3, any finite linear combination of elements of \(U\) must be in \(\text{span}(U)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Exercises 3 Exercises

    1.

    Describe the following sets in your own words:
    🔗
    • \(\displaystyle \text{span}((1,0))\)
      🔗
    • \(\displaystyle \text{span}((0,1))\)
      🔗
    • \(\text{span}((0,1,0), (0,0,1))\text{?}\)
      🔗
    • \(\displaystyle \text{span}((0,1,0))\)
      🔗
    • \(\text{span}((1,-1,0), (1,0,-1), (0,1,-1))\text{.}\)
      🔗
    🔗
    Recall that to denote an arbitrary function from \(\mathbb{R}\) to \(\mathbb{R}\text{,}\) we say let \(f: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}\text{.}\)
    🔗

    2.

    Show that the function \(f_{\vec{0}}: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}\) defined by \(f_{\vec{0}}(x) = 0\) for all \(x \in \mathbb{R}\) is the zero vector in the vector space of real-valued functions.
    🔗
    🔗

    3.

    Fix \(m, n \in \mathbb{Z}_{> 0}\) (positive integers). Show that \(m \times n\) matrices whose rows and columns all sum to \(0\) are a linear subspace of the vector space of all \(m \times n\) matrices.
    🔗
    🔗
    Recall the for two sets \(S\) and \(T\text{,}\) the intersection \(S \cap T\) is the set of elements in both \(S\) and \(T\text{,}\) and the union \(S \cup T\) is the set of elements in at least one of \(S\) or \(T\text{.}\)
    🔗

    4.

    Let \(U\) and \(W\) be linear subspaces of a vector space \(V\text{.}\) Prove that \(U \cap W\) is also a subspace. What about \(U \cup W\text{?}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    5.

    Give examples of subsets of a vector space closed under addition but not scalar multiplication and the reverse.
