    Subsection 1.2 Regressing

    A classic tool used throughout the sciences that you may already be familiar with is linear regression. For this you have a set of \(m\) points \((x_{1},y_{1}), \dots, (x_{m},y_{m})\) in \(\mathbb{R}^{2}\) and you want to find the line of best fit for those points.
    🔗
    What does it mean to fit best? We want to minimize the error of our predictions. For example, suppose we get the line \(y = \beta_{0} + \beta_{1} x\text{.}\) Then the error at the point \((x_{i}, y_{i})\) is
    \begin{equation*} |y_{i} - (\beta_{0} + \beta_{1}x_{i})|\text{.} \end{equation*}
    When minimizing error over many points, there’s a choice here. It turns out we can think of this from a geometric perspective. Namely, we can store our error terms in a vector:
    \begin{equation*} \vec{r} = (y_{1} - \beta_{0} -\beta_{1}x_{1},y_{2} - \beta_{0} -\beta_{1}x_{2}, \dots, y_{m} -\beta_{0}-\beta_{1}x_{m})\text{.} \end{equation*}
    In other words,
    \begin{equation*} r_{i} = y_{i} - \beta_{0} -\beta_{1} x_{i}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    The vector \(\vec{r}\) is called the residual vector. When we are minimizing error, we think of this geometrically as minimizing the distance from \(\vec{r}\) to the origin. From the distance equation, or really, the Pythagorean theorem,
    \begin{equation*} \text{dist}(\vec{r},\vec{0}) = \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{m} r_{i}^{2}} = \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{m} (y_{i}-\beta_{0}-\beta_{1}x_{i})^{2}}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    The notation we use for this is called a norm, and it’s like an absolute value with \(2\) bars:
    \begin{equation*} \|\vec{r}\| = \text{dist}(\vec{r}, \vec{0}) = \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{m} r_{i}^{2}}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Minimizing the square root of a non-negative function is the same as just minimizing the square. Thus, we want to solve the following optimization problem:
    \begin{equation*} \min_{\beta_{0}, \beta_{1}} \sum_{i=1}^{m} (y_{i}-\beta_{0}-\beta_{1}x_{i})^{2}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    We can further rewrite this expression. Define the matrix:
    \begin{equation*} X = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & x_{1} \\1 & x_{2} \\ \vdots & \vdots \\ 1 & x_{m}\end{pmatrix}\text{,} \end{equation*}
    and define
    \begin{equation*} \vec{y} = \begin{pmatrix} y_{1} \\ y_{2} \\ \vdots \\y_{m} \end{pmatrix} \text{ and } \vec{\beta} = \begin{pmatrix} \beta_{0}\\\beta_{1} \end{pmatrix}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    Then we can rewrite the residuals more compactly:
    \begin{equation*} \vec{r} = \vec{y} - X \vec{\beta}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    Note that \(\vec{y}\) and \(X\) are fixed as part of our input data. The only thing that varies is \(\vec{\beta}\text{.}\) This gives rise to a general problem.
    🔗

    Subsection 1.3 Best Approximations for Solving Linear Systems

    We have discussed ad nauseam that a system of linear equations
    \begin{equation*} A \vec{x} = \vec{b} \end{equation*}
    need not have a solution. However, what should we do if there is no solution? Can we please just give up? No! Of course not.
    🔗
    What we could instead ask is how close are we to having a solution. Concretely we have a solution if
    \begin{equation*} A \vec{x} - \vec{b} = \vec{0}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    We could instead ask to minimize \(\|A\vec{x}-\vec{b}\|\text{.}\) To do this, we minimize the distance to the origin expressed as
    \begin{equation*} \min_{\vec{x} \text{ in }\mathbb{R}^{n}} \|\vec{b} - A \vec{x}\|\text{.} \end{equation*}
    If we can solve this, we can do linear regression and find a line of best fit. Thus, this is our goal. We can reformulate this a bit further in a way that will be helpful. Namely, vectors of the form \(A \vec{x}\) are exactly the column span of \(A\text{.}\) In other words,
    \begin{equation*} \min_{\vec{x} \text{ in }\mathbb{R}^{n}} \|\vec{b} - A\vec{x}\| = \min_{\vec{y} \text{ in } \text{ColSpan}(A)} \| \vec{b} - \vec{y}\|\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Thus, another way to view this problem is that we have a vector \(\vec{b}\) and a linear subspace \(W\text{.}\) How can we find the closest vector to \(\vec{b}\) in that subspace? Our next goal is to solve this problem, and for it, we will need to develop some theory.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.4 The Gram-Schmidt Process

    Suppose I have a basis for a subspace of \(\mathbb{R}^{n}\text{:}\) \(\vec{v}_{1}, \dots, \vec{v}_{k}\text{.}\) Let \(\vec{u} \in \mathbb{R}^{n}\text{.}\) Then if we’re very lucky \(\vec{u}\) is in that subspace, but what if it is not? How do we find the distance to that subspace? It turns out there is a recipe for doing this called the Gram-Schmidt Process. The idea is as follows: Find a basis to write every vector in for which computing distances is easier.
    🔗
    Let \(\vec{v}_{1}, \dots, \vec{v}_{n}\) be vectors in \(\mathbb{R}^{n}\text{.}\) We call \(\vec{v}_{1},\dots, \vec{v}_{n}\) an orthonormal basis if
    \begin{equation*} \vec{v}_{i} \cdot \vec{v}_{j} = \begin{cases} 1 &\text{if }i=j\\0 &\text{otherwise.} \end{cases} \end{equation*}
    The reason this is a basis is that if we let \(A\) be the matrix with columns \(\vec{v}_{1}, \dots, \vec{v}_{n}\) and let \(B = A^{\intercal} A\text{,}\) then by definition of matrix multiplication, \(B_{i,j} = \vec{v}_{i} \cdot \vec{v}_{j}\text{.}\) Then
    \begin{equation*} B_{i,j} = \vec{v}_{i} \cdot \vec{v}_{j} = \begin{cases} 1 &\text{if }i=j\\0 &\text{otherwise.} \end{cases} \end{equation*}
    Thus, \(B\) has ones along the diagonal and zeroes otherwise and is therefore an identity matrix. Thus, \(A^{-1} = A^{\intercal}\text{.}\) We can recall from our group theory unit that we call such a matrix orthogonal.
    🔗

    Theorem 1.1.

    For any basis \(\vec{v}_{1}, \vec{v}_{2},\dots, \vec{v}_{n}\) of \(\mathbb{R}^{n}\text{,}\) there exists an orthonormal basis \(\vec{u}_{1}, \dots, \vec{u}_{n}\) such that
    \begin{equation*} \text{span}(\vec{v}_{1}, \dots, \vec{v}_{k}) = \text{span}(\vec{u}_{1},\dots,\vec{u}_{k}) \end{equation*}
    for all \(1 \leq k \leq n\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    First, we let
    \begin{equation*} \vec{u}_{1} = \frac{\vec{v}_{1}}{\sqrt{\vec{v}_{1} \cdot \vec{v}_{1}}}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    Then for \(k \geq 2\) we define
    \begin{equation*} \vec{w}_{k} = \vec{v}_{k} - \sum_{i=1}^{k-1} (\vec{u}_{i}\cdot \vec{v}_{k} )\vec{u}_{i}\text{,} \end{equation*}
    and
    \begin{equation*} \vec{u}_{k} = \frac{\vec{w}_{k}}{\sqrt{\vec{w}_{k} \cdot \vec{w}_{k}}}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    Then for \(j \lt k\text{,}\) assuming \(\vec{u}_{1},\ldots,\vec{u}_{k-1}\) are orthonormal,
    \begin{align*} \vec{u}_{j} \cdot \vec{w}_{k} \amp = \vec{u}_{j} \cdot \left(\vec{v}_{k} -\sum_{i=1}^{k-1} (\vec{u}_{i}\cdot \vec{v}_{k} )\vec{u}_{i}\right)\\ \amp = \vec{u}_{j} \cdot \vec{v}_{k} -\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}(\vec{u}_{i}\cdot \vec{v}_{k} )(\vec{u}_{j} \cdot \vec{u}_{i})\\ \amp = \vec{u}_{j} \cdot \vec{v}_{k} - (\vec{u}_{j} \cdot \vec{v}_{k})(\vec{u}_{j}\cdot \vec{u}_{j})\\ \amp = 0\text{.} \end{align*}
    Since \(\vec{u}_{k}\) is just a rescaling of \(\vec{w}_{k}\text{,}\) it follows that \(\vec{u}_{j} \cdot \vec{u}_{k} = 0\text{.}\) By construction, \(\vec{u}_{k} \cdot \vec{u}_{k} = 1\text{.}\) That the spans coincide follows by construction.
    🔗
    🔗
    The Gram-Schmidt process is the algorithm used to make this happen:
    1. Start with \(\vec{v}_{1},\dots, \vec{v}_{n}\text{.}\)
      🔗
    2. Iteratively replace \(\vec{v}_{k}\) with \(\vec{u}_{k}=\frac{\vec{w}_{k}}{\sqrt{\vec{w}_{k} \cdot \vec{w}_{k}}}\text{,}\) where
      \begin{equation*} \vec{w}_{k} = \vec{v}_{k} - \sum_{i=1}^{k-1}(\vec{u}_{i}\cdot \vec{v}_{k})\vec{u}_{i}\text{.} \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    Then \(\vec{u}_{1},\dots,\vec{u}_{n}\) is an orthonormal basis.
    🔗
    The beauty of having an orthonormal basis is that distances are easy to compute. To start, as discussed above, the distance from a vector \(\vec{a}\) to the origin is given by the distance formula:
    \begin{equation*} \|\vec{a}\| = \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n} a_{i}^{2}} = \sqrt{\vec{a} \cdot \vec{a}}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    Now let’s use an orthonormal basis \(\vec{u}_{1},\dots,\vec{u}_{n}\text{.}\) Then
    \begin{equation*} \vec{a} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} b_{i} \vec{u}_{i}\text{,} \end{equation*}
    where \(b_{i} = \vec{a} \cdot \vec{u}_{i}\text{.}\) Then
    \begin{align*} \|\vec{a}\|^{2} \amp = \vec{a} \cdot \vec{a}\\ \amp = \left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}b_{i} \vec{u}_{i}\right) \cdot\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n} b_{j}\vec{u}_{j} \right)\\ \amp = \sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n} b_{i}b_{j}(\vec{u}_{i} \cdot \vec{u}_{j})\\ \amp = \sum_{i=1}^{n} b_{i}^{2}(\vec{u}_{i}\cdot\vec{u}_{i})\\ \amp = \sum_{i=1}^{n} b_{i}^{2}\\ \amp = \sum_{i=1}^{n} (\vec{u}_{i} \cdot \vec{a})^{2}\text{.} \end{align*}
    Thus,
    \begin{equation*} \|\vec{a}\| = \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n} (\vec{u}_{i} \cdot \vec{a})^{2}} = \|C\vec{a}\|\text{,} \end{equation*}
    where \(C\) is the matrix with rows \(\vec{u}_{1}, \dots, \vec{u}_{n}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    This actually allows us to solve the distance to a subspace minimization problem. Namely, the subspace is \(\text{span}(\vec{v}_{1}, \dots, \vec{v}_{k})\text{.}\)
    1. Extend \(\vec{v}_{1},\dots,\vec{v}_{k}\) to a basis of all of \(\mathbb{R}^{n}\text{,}\) \(\vec{v}_{1},\dots, \vec{v}_{n}\text{.}\)
      🔗
    2. Apply Gram-Schmidt to \(\vec{v}_{1},\dots, \vec{v}_{n}\) to get \(\vec{u}_{1},\dots, \vec{u}_{n}\text{.}\)
      🔗
    3. Take the matrix \(U\) with rows \(\vec{u}_{k+1}, \dots, \vec{u}_{n}\text{.}\) Then \(\|U \vec{b}\|\) is the distance, and \(\vec{b} - U^{\intercal} U\vec{b}\) gives the closest vector in that subspace.
      🔗
