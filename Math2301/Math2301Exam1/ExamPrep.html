    Section 1 MATH 2301 Exam 1 Prep

    Subsection 1.1 Cake or Fake

    You need to prove or disprove that something is a linear transformation or linear subspace.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 1.1.

    Is \(T:\mathbb{R}^{3} \to \mathbb{R}^{3}\) defined by \(T(x,y,z) = (z,x,y)\) a linear transformation?
    🔗
    Solution.
    Yes it is given by multiplication by the matrix:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} T\left(\begin{bmatrix} x\\y\\z \end{bmatrix} \right) = \begin{bmatrix} z \\ x\\ y\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \amp 0 \amp 1 \\ 1 \amp 0 \amp 0 \\ 0 \amp 1 \amp 0 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x\\y\\z \end{bmatrix} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    and is therefore a linear transformation.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 1.2.

    Let \(S\) be the set of polynomials with roots at \(0, 1,\) and \(-1\) (i.e., all polynomials \(f\) such that \(f(1) = f(0) = f(-1) = 0\)). Is \(S\) a linear subspace?
    🔗
    Solution.
    Recall that for any \(c \in \mathbb{R}\text{,}\) the evaluation map \(\text{ev}_{c}: \mathbb{R}[x] \to \mathbb{R}\) defined by \(\text{ev}_{c}(f) = f(c)\) is a linear transformation for each \(c\text{.}\) Thus, the map \(f \mapsto (f(-1), f(0), f(1))\) is a linear transformation and by definition has kernel given by \(S\text{.}\) Therefore, \(S\) is a linear subspace.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 1.3.

    Let \(W_{\geq 0}\) be the set of non-negative circulations of the cycle graph \(G = (\{1,2,3\}, \{(1,2), (2,3), (3,1)\})\text{.}\) Is \(W_{\geq 0}\) a linear subspace?
    🔗
    Solution.
    It is not. Note that \(G\) contains a cycle \((1,2), (2,3), (3,1)\text{,}\) so \(W_{\geq 0}\) contains the indicator vector of that cycle. However, multiplying by \(-1\) yields a vector with negative edge weights, which is, by definition, not a non-negative circulation. Thus, it is not closed under scalar multiplication.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.2 New from Old

    I will ask you to prove that a way of combining two things leads to another thing of the same type.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 1.4.

    Let \(T_{1}: U \to V\) and \(T_{2}: U \to V\) be linear transformations. Show that \(T = T_{1} + T_{2}\) is a linear transformation.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Let \(\vec{x}, \vec{y} \in U\text{.}\) Then, since \(T_{1}\) and \(T_{2}\) are linear transformations,
    🔗
    \begin{align*} T(\vec{x} +\vec{y}) \amp= T_{1}(\vec{x}+ \vec{y}) + T_{2}(\vec{x}+\vec{y})\\ \amp= T_{1}(\vec{x}) + T_{1}(\vec{y}) + T_{2}(\vec{x}) + T_{2}(\vec{y})\\ \amp= (T_{1}(\vec{x}) +T_{2}(\vec{x})) + (T_{1}(\vec{y}) + T_{2}(\vec{y}))\\ \amp= T(\vec{x}) + T(\vec{y})\text{.} \end{align*}
    🔗
    Let \(\lambda \in \mathbb{R}\text{.}\) Then
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} T(\lambda \vec{x}) = T_{1}(\lambda \vec{x}) + T_{2}(\lambda \vec{x}) = \lambda T_{1}(\vec{x}) + \lambda T_{2}(\vec{x}) = \lambda T(\vec{x})\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Therefore, \(T\) is a linear transformation.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 1.5.

    Let \(V\) be a vector space, and let \(S\) and \(T\) be subspaces of \(V\text{.}\) Show that \(S \cap T = \{\vec{x}: \vec{x} \in S \text{ and } \vec{x} \in T\}\) is a linear subspace.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Note that since \(S\) and \(T\) are subspaces of \(V\text{,}\) both contain \(\vec{0}\text{,}\) so \(\vec{0} \in S \cap T\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Let \(\vec{u}, \vec{v} \in S \cap T\text{.}\) Then \(\vec{u}, \vec{v} \in S\text{,}\) so since \(S\) is a linear subspace, \(\vec{u}+ \vec{v} \in S\text{.}\) By similar reasoning, \(\vec{u} + \vec{v} \in T\text{.}\) Therefore, \(\vec{u} + \vec{v} \in S \cap T\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Let \(c \in \mathbb{R}\text{.}\) Then, since \(S\) is a linear subspace, \(c \vec{u} \in S\text{.}\) Similarly, \(c \vec{u} \in T\text{,}\) so \(c \vec{u} \in S \cap T\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Therefore, \(S \cap T\) is a linear subspace.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 1.6.

    Let \(T_{1}: U \to V\) and \(T_{2}: V \to W\) be linear transformations. Show that \(T_{2} \circ T_{1}\) is a linear transformation.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Let \(\vec{x}, \vec{y} \in U\text{.}\) Then
    🔗
    \begin{align*} (T_{2} \circ T_{1})(\vec{x} + \vec{y}) \amp= T_{2}(T_{1}(\vec{x}) + T_{1}(\vec{y}))\\ \amp= (T_{2}\circ T_{1})(\vec{x}) + (T_{2} \circ T_{1})(\vec{y})\text{.} \end{align*}
    🔗
    Let \(c \in \mathbb{R}\text{.}\) Then
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} (T_{2} \circ T_{1})(c \vec{x}) = T_{2}(cT_{1}(\vec{x})) = c (T_{2}\circ T_{1})(\vec{x})\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Thus, \(T_{2} \circ T_{1}\) is a linear transformation.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.3 Span, Independence, Bases

    You are given some information about spanning or independence, and you need to make some conclusions based off of it.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 1.7.

    Let \(T: U \to V\) be a linear transformation. Show that if \(S \subseteq U\) is spanning, then \(T(S) = \{T(\vec{x}): \vec{x} \in S\}\) spans \(\text{Im}(T)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Let \(\vec{y} \in \text{Im}(T)\text{.}\) Then \(\vec{y} = T(\vec{x})\text{.}\) Since \(S\) is spanning, \(\vec{x} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} a_{i} \vec{x}_{i}\) for some \(a_{1}, \dots, a_{n} \in \mathbb{R}\) and \(\vec{x}_{1}, \dots, \vec{x}_{n} \in S\text{.}\) Therefore,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \vec{y} = T(\vec{x}) = T\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n} a_{i} \vec{x}_{i} \right) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} a_{i} T(\vec{x}_{i}) \in \text{span}(T(S))\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Therefore, \(T(S)\) spans \(\text{Im}(T)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 1.8.

    Let \(S = \{\vec{x}_{1}, \dots, \vec{x}_{n}\} \subseteq V\) be a subset of a vector space of size \(n\text{.}\) Define \(T: \mathbb{R}^{n} \to V\) by
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} T(\vec{y}) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} y_{i} \vec{x}_{i}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Show that if \(\text{ker}(T) \neq \{\vec{0}\}\text{,}\) then the vectors in \(S\) are linearly dependent.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Since \(\text{ker}(T) \neq \{\vec{0}\}\text{,}\) there exists \(\vec{y} \neq \vec{0}\) such that \(\vec{y} \in \ker(T)\text{.}\) For such a \(\vec{y}\text{,}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \vec{0} = T(\vec{y}) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} y_{i} \vec{x}_{i}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Since \(\vec{y} \neq \vec{0}\text{,}\) not all \(y_{i}\) are \(0\text{,}\) meaning that the \(\vec{x}_{i}\) are, by definition, not linearly independent.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 1.9.

    Show that there is no linear transformation \(T: \mathbb{R}^{2} \to \mathbb{R}^{3}\) such that \(\text{Im}(T) = \mathbb{R}^{3}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Suppose for the sake of contradiction that \(\text{Im}(T) = \mathbb{R}^{3}\text{.}\) By the rank-nullity theorem,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 3 = \text{dim}(\mathbb{R}^{3}) = \text{dim}(\text{Im}(T)) = \text{rank}(T) \leq \text{rank}(T) + \text{nullity}(T) = \text{dim}(\mathbb{R}^{2}) = 2\text{,} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    a contradiction.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.4 ... And its Applications

    Checkpoint 1.10.

    In Dungeons and Dragons, you roll a twenty-sided die to make decisions. If you roll a \(20\text{,}\) you get a critical hit, and something amazing happens. If you roll a \(1\text{,}\) you get a critical failure and something terrible happens. Assuming the die is fair, how many times do you expect to roll it until you roll your first critical hit or failure?
    🔗
    Solution.
    The probability of having a critical hit or failure is \(2/20 = 1/10\) on any roll. We will call being in either case a critical roll. Let \(X\) be the random variable giving the number of rolls until the first critical hit or failure.
    🔗
    Then \(X = Y+ Z\text{,}\) where \(Y\) is \(1\) if the first roll is critical and \(0\) otherwise, and \(Z\) is \(0\) if the first roll is critical and otherwise is the number of rolls until the first critical roll. Then \(\mathbb{E}[X] = \mathbb{E}[Y] + \mathbb{E}[Z]\) by linearity of expectation. Furthermore, \(\mathbb{E}[Y] = 1/10\) as there is a probability of \(1/10\) of getting a critical roll on the first roll, and \(\mathbb{E}[Z] = 9/10(1 + \mathbb{E}[X])\) as there is a probability of \(9/10\) of the first roll not being critical and the probability distribution after that point is the same as \(X\) but with one additional roll needed.
    🔗
    Then
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \mathbb{E}[X] = 1/10 + 9/10(\mathbb{E}[X] + 1) = 1 + 9/10 \mathbb{E}[X]\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Thus,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} (1/10) \mathbb{E}[X] = 1\text{,} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    so \(\mathbb{E}[X] = 10\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 1.12.

    Let \(G = (N,E)\) be a directed graph with weight function \(\vec{w}\text{.}\) Show that the sum of the total in-flow at each vertex is equal to the sum of the total out-flow at each vertex.
    🔗
    Solution.
    The weight of each edge is counted in precisely one in-flow and one out-flow, so the sum of all in-flows is the total weight across all edges, and so is the sum of all out-flows. Hence, the sum of all in-flows is equal to the sum of all out-flows.
