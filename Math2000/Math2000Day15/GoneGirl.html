    Section 1 Completely Ordinary Equations

    Subsection 1.1 Review

    What does it mean for a basis to be orthonormal?
    🔗

    Checkpoint 1.1.

    Prove that the columns of an \(n \times n\) matrix \(A\) form an orthonormal basis if and only if \(A^{\intercal} A = I_{n}\text{,}\) where \(I_{n}\) is the \(n \times n\) identity matrix.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.2 A Better Way

    Last time we reduced linear regression to the following optimization problem:
    \begin{equation*} \min_{\vec{x} \in \mathbb{R}^{n}} \|A \vec{x} - \vec{b}\| \end{equation*}
    for an \(m \times n\) matrix \(A\) and \(\vec{b} \in \mathbb{R}^{m}\text{.}\) We discussed a way to solve this using Gram-Schmidt, but as you may have discovered from the exercises, doing Gram-Schmidt is kind of a pain. Is there a simpler way?
    🔗
    It turns out you can represent this by solving a linear system.
    🔗

    Theorem 1.2.

    Let \(\vec{y} \in \mathbb{R}^{n}\) be a solution to the linear system \(A^{\intercal} A \vec{x} = A^{\intercal} \vec{b}\text{.}\) Then \(\vec{y}\) is a minimizer of \(\|A \vec{x} - \vec{b}\|\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    The equations \(A^{\intercal} A \vec{x} = A^{\intercal} \vec{b}\) are called the normal equations. It is not obvious that they would even have any solution. To prove this, we need to build more theory.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.3 Orthogonal Decomposition

    Let \(W\) be a linear subspace of an inner product space \(V\text{.}\) Define its orthogonal complement
    \begin{equation*} W^{\perp} = \{\vec{v} \in V: \langle \vec{v},\vec{w}\rangle = 0 \text{ for all }\vec{w} \in W\}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Theorem 1.3.

    Let \(W\) be a linear subspace of a finite-dimensional real inner product space \(V\text{.}\) Then
    1. \(W^{\perp}\) is a subspace of \(V\text{.}\)
      🔗
    2. \(W + W^{\perp} = V\text{.}\)
      🔗
    3. \(W \cap W^{\perp} = \{\vec{0}\}\text{.}\)
      🔗
    4. Any vector \(\vec{u} \in V\) can be written uniquely as \(\vec{u} = \vec{w} + \vec{w}'\) for some \(\vec{w} \in W\) and \(\vec{w}' \in W^{\perp}\text{.}\)
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    For (a), let \(\vec{x}, \vec{y} \in W^{\perp}\) and \(\lambda \in \mathbb{R}\text{.}\) Let \(\vec{z} \in W\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Show that \(\langle \vec{x} + \vec{y}, \vec{z}\rangle = 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Show that \(\langle \lambda \vec{x}, \vec{z}\rangle = 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Explain why \(W^{\perp}\) is a linear subspace.
    🔗
    🔗
    For (b), take an orthonormal basis \(\vec{b}_{1}, \dots, \vec{b}_{n}\) of \(V\) such that \(\vec{b}_{1}, \dots, \vec{b}_{k}\) is an orthonormal basis for \(W\text{.}\) This exists by the Gram-Schmidt procedure.
    🔗
    Then let \(\vec{v} \in V\text{.}\) Since this is a basis,
    \begin{equation*} \vec{v} = \sum_{i=1}^{k} a_{i} \vec{b}_{i} + \sum_{j=k+1}^{n} a_{j}\vec{b}_{j}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    Explain why \(\vec{x} = \sum_{i=1}^{k} a_{i} \vec{b}_{i} \in W\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Note that \(\vec{b}_{1}, \dots, \vec{b}_{k}\) is a basis for \(W\text{.}\) Explain why \(\langle \vec{b}_{j}, \vec{b}_{i} \rangle = 0\) for all \(1 \leq i \leq k\) and \(k+1 \leq j \leq n\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Use this observation to argue that \(\vec{b}_{j} \in W^{\perp}\) for all \(k+1 \leq j \leq n\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Explain why \(\vec{y} = \sum_{j=k+1}^{n}a_{j} \vec{b}_{j} \in W^{\perp}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Thus, \(\vec{v} = \vec{x} + \vec{y}\text{,}\) where \(\vec{x} \in W\) and \(\vec{y} \in W^{\perp}\text{,}\) so \(V \subseteq W + W^{\perp}\text{.}\) Since \(W + W^{\perp} \subseteq V\text{,}\) \(W + W^{\perp} = V\text{.}\)
    🔗
    For (c), let \(\vec{z} \in W \cap W^{\perp}\text{.}\) Since \(\vec{z} \in V\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} \vec{z} = \sum_{i=1}^{k} c_{i} \vec{b}_{i} + \sum_{j=k+1}^{n} c_{j} \vec{b}_{j}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    Explain why \(\vec{z} \in W^{\perp}\) implies \(\langle \vec{z}, \vec{b}_{i} \rangle = 0\) for all \(1 \leq i \leq k\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Explain why \(\vec{z} \in W\) implies \(\langle \vec{z}, \vec{b}_{j} \rangle = 0\) for all \(k+1 \leq j \leq n\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Therefore,
    \begin{align*} \|\vec{z}\|^{2} \amp = \langle \vec{z}, \vec{z} \rangle\\ \amp = \left\langle \vec{z}, \sum_{i=1}^{n} c_{i} \vec{b}_{i} \right\rangle\\ \amp = \sum_{i=1}^{n} c_{i} \langle \vec{z}, \vec{b}_{i}\rangle\\ \amp = \sum_{i=1}^{n} c_{i} 0\\ \amp = 0\text{.} \end{align*}
    Explain why this means \(\vec{z} = \vec{0}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Therefore, \(W \cap W^{\perp} = \{\vec{0}\}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    For (d), suppose that \(\vec{u} = \vec{x}_{1} + \vec{x}_{2} = \vec{y}_{1} + \vec{y}_{2}\) for some \(\vec{x}_{1}, \vec{y}_{1} \in W\) and \(\vec{x}_{2}, \vec{y}_{2} \in W^{\perp}\text{.}\) Rearranging, \(\vec{x}_{1}- \vec{y}_{1} = \vec{y}_{2} -\vec{x}_{2}\text{.}\) Explain why \(\vec{x}_{1} -\vec{y}_{1} \in W\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Explain why \(\vec{y}_{2} - \vec{x}_{2} \in W^{\perp}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Thus, \(\vec{x}_{1} - \vec{y}_{1}, \vec{y}_{2} - \vec{x}_{2} \in W \cap W^{\perp}\text{.}\) Explain why this means \(\vec{x}_{1} = \vec{y}_{1}\) and \(\vec{x}_{2} = \vec{y}_{2}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Therefore the orthogonal decomposition is unique.
    🔗
    🔗
    This decomposition gives us a canonical way to isolate the closest point.
    🔗

    Theorem 1.4.

    Let \(W\) be a subspace of an inner product space \(V\text{,}\) and let \(\vec{v} \in V\text{.}\) Let \(\vec{v} = \vec{a} + \vec{b}\text{,}\) where \(\vec{a} \in W\) and \(\vec{b} \in W^{\perp}\text{.}\) Then \(\vec{a}\) is the closest point in \(W\) to \(\vec{v}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    Let \(\vec{u} \in W\text{.}\) Then, by the Pythagorean theorem,
    \begin{align*} \|\vec{u} - \vec{v}\|^{2} \amp = \| \vec{u} -\vec{a} -\vec{b}\|^{2}\\ \amp = \|(\vec{u}-\vec{a})-\vec{b}\|^{2}\\ \amp = \|\vec{u}-\vec{a}\|^{2} + \|\vec{b}\|^{2}\\ \amp \geq \|\vec{b}\|^{2}\\ \amp = \|(\vec{a}+\vec{b})-\vec{a}\|^{2}\\ \amp = \|\vec{v}-\vec{a}\|^{2}\text{.} \end{align*}
    Therefore, \(\|\vec{u}-\vec{v}\|\) is minimized precisely when \(\vec{u} = \vec{a}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.4 Proving the Better Way

    We use this observation to verify that the normal equations are doing precisely what we want them to be doing.
    🔗

    Proof. (Theorem 1.2)
    We will do the proof in two steps. First, I will argue that a minimizer is a solution to the normal equations \(A^{\intercal} A \vec{x} = A^{\intercal} \vec{b}\text{.}\) This in particular tells you that a solution always exists. Second we argue that any solution will be a closest point.
    🔗
    Let \(W\) be the span of the columns of \(A\text{.}\) Then let \(\vec{b} = \vec{y} + \vec{z}\) be the orthogonal decomposition of \(\vec{b}\) with \(\vec{y} \in W\) and \(\vec{z} \in W^{\perp}\text{.}\) Then \(\vec{y}\) is the closest point to \(\vec{b}\) in \(W\text{.}\) Furthermore, \(\vec{y} \in \text{span}(\text{cols}(A))\text{,}\) so \(\vec{y} = A \vec{x}\) for some \(\vec{x} \in \mathbb{R}^{n}\text{.}\) Then
    \begin{equation*} A^{\intercal}(A\vec{x} -\vec{b}) = -A^{\intercal} \vec{z} \end{equation*}
    Explain why \(\vec{z} \in W^{\perp}\) implies that \(A^{\intercal} \vec{z} = \vec{0}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Thus, \(A^{\intercal} (A \vec{x} - \vec{b}) = \vec{0}\text{,}\) so \(A^{\intercal} A \vec{x} = A^{\intercal}\vec{b}\text{.}\) It follows that if \(A\vec{x}\) is the closest point in the image of \(A\) to \(\vec{b}\text{,}\) then \(\vec{x}\) is a solution to that equation. In particular, a solution always exists.
    🔗
    What remains is to show that any solution arises in that way. Let \(\vec{x}\) be a solution to the normal equation. We want to argue that \(A\vec{x}\) is the closest point in \(W\) to \(\vec{b}\text{.}\) For this, we work in reverse. We can rewrite \(A^{\intercal} A \vec{x} = A^{\intercal} \vec{b}\) as \(A^{\intercal}(A\vec{x} - \vec{b}) = \vec{0}\text{.}\) Thus, \(A\vec{x} -\vec{b}\) is in \(\ker(A^{\intercal})\) and thus in \(W^{\perp}\text{.}\) Since \(W^{\perp}\) is a subspace, \(\vec{b} - A \vec{x} \in W^{\perp}\text{,}\) so \(\vec{b} = (\vec{b}-A\vec{x}) + A\vec{x}\) is the orthogonal decomposition of \(\vec{b}\text{.}\) It follows that the closest point to \(\vec{b}\) in \(W\) is \(A\vec{x}\text{,}\) which completes the proof.
