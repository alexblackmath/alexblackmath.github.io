    🔗

    Section 1 Exam 2

    Subsection 1.1 Cake or Fake

    Prove or disprove that each object is a linear transformation or a linear subspace, as appropriate.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 1.1.

    Is \(T:\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}\) defined by \(T(x,y,z)=(z,x,y)\) a linear transformation?
    🔗
    Solution.
    Yes. Let \(\mathbf{u}=(x_1,y_1,z_1)\text{,}\) \(\mathbf{v}=(x_2,y_2,z_2)\text{,}\) and let \(a,b\in\mathbb{R}\text{.}\) Then
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} T(a\mathbf{u}+b\mathbf{v}) =(az_1+bz_2,ax_1+bx_2,ay_1+by_2) =aT(\mathbf{u})+bT(\mathbf{v}). \end{equation*}
    Therefore \(T\) is linear.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 1.2.

    Let \(S\) be the set of real polynomials that vanish at \(0\text{,}\) \(1\text{,}\) and \(-1\text{;}\) that is,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} S=\{f\in\mathbb{R}[x]:f(1)=f(0)=f(-1)=0\}. \end{equation*}
    Is \(S\) a linear subspace of \(\mathbb{R}[x]\text{?}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Yes. The zero polynomial belongs to \(S\text{.}\) If \(f,g\in S\) and \(a,b\in\mathbb{R}\text{,}\) then for each \(r\in\{-1,0,1\}\text{,}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} (af+bg)(r)=af(r)+bg(r)=0. \end{equation*}
    Thus \(af+bg\in S\text{,}\) so \(S\) is a subspace. Equivalently, every polynomial in \(S\) is divisible by \(x(x-1)(x+1)\text{,}\) so
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} S=x(x-1)(x+1)\mathbb{R}[x]. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 1.3.

    Let \(W_{\geq 0}\) be the set of nonnegative circulations on the directed cycle graph
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} G=(\{1,2,3\},\{(1,2),(2,3),(3,1)\}). \end{equation*}
    Is \(W_{\geq 0}\) a linear subspace?
    🔗
    Solution.
    No. The circulation that assigns weight \(1\) to each of the three edges is in \(W_{\geq 0}\text{.}\) Multiplying it by \(-1\) gives a circulation with weight \(-1\) on every edge, which is not nonnegative. Hence \(W_{\geq 0}\) is not closed under scalar multiplication and therefore is not a linear subspace.
    🔗

    Subsection 1.2 New from Old

    Prove that each indicated way of combining two objects produces another object of the same type.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 1.4.

    Let \(T_1:U\to V\) and \(T_2:U\to V\) be linear transformations. Define \(T=T_1+T_2\) by \(T(\mathbf{x})=T_1(\mathbf{x})+T_2(\mathbf{x})\text{.}\) Show that \(T\) is a linear transformation.
    🔗
    Solution.
    For \(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\in U\) and scalars \(a,b\text{,}\) linearity of \(T_1\) and \(T_2\) gives
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} T(a\mathbf{u}+b\mathbf{v}) &=T_1(a\mathbf{u}+b\mathbf{v})+T_2(a\mathbf{u}+b\mathbf{v})\\ &=aT_1(\mathbf{u})+bT_1(\mathbf{v})+aT_2(\mathbf{u})+bT_2(\mathbf{v})\\ &=aT(\mathbf{u})+bT(\mathbf{v}). \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    Thus \(T\) is linear.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 1.5.

    Let \(V\) be a vector space, and let \(S\) and \(T\) be subspaces of \(V\text{.}\) Show that
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} S\cap T=\{\mathbf{x}:\mathbf{x}\in S\text{ and }\mathbf{x}\in T\} \end{equation*}
    is a linear subspace of \(V\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Because \(S\) and \(T\) are subspaces, \(\mathbf{0}\) lies in both, so \(\mathbf{0}\in S\cap T\text{.}\) If \(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in S\cap T\) and \(a,b\) are scalars, then \(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in S\) and \(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in T\text{.}\) Since both sets are subspaces, \(a\mathbf{x}+b\mathbf{y}\) belongs to both \(S\) and \(T\text{.}\) Therefore \(a\mathbf{x}+b\mathbf{y}\in S\cap T\text{,}\) proving that \(S\cap T\) is a subspace.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 1.6.

    Let \(T_1:U\to V\) and \(T_2:V\to W\) be linear transformations. Show that \(T_2\circ T_1\) is a linear transformation.
    🔗
    Solution.
    For \(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\in U\) and scalars \(a,b\text{,}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} (T_2\circ T_1)(a\mathbf{u}+b\mathbf{v}) &=T_2\bigl(T_1(a\mathbf{u}+b\mathbf{v})\bigr)\\ &=T_2\bigl(aT_1(\mathbf{u})+bT_1(\mathbf{v})\bigr)\\ &=aT_2(T_1(\mathbf{u}))+bT_2(T_1(\mathbf{v}))\\ &=a(T_2\circ T_1)(\mathbf{u})+b(T_2\circ T_1)(\mathbf{v}). \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    Hence \(T_2\circ T_1\) is linear.
    🔗

    Subsection 1.3 Span, Independence, Bases

    Use the given information about spanning or linear independence to draw the requested conclusions.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 1.7.

    Let \(T:U\to V\) be a linear transformation. Show that if \(S\subseteq U\) spans \(U\text{,}\) then
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} T(S)=\{T(\mathbf{x}):\mathbf{x}\in S\} \end{equation*}
    spans \(\operatorname{Im}(T)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Let \(\mathbf{y}\in\operatorname{Im}(T)\text{.}\) Then \(\mathbf{y}=T(\mathbf{x})\) for some \(\mathbf{x}\in U\text{.}\) Since \(S\) spans \(U\text{,}\) there are vectors \(\mathbf{s}_1,\ldots,\mathbf{s}_k\in S\) and scalars \(a_1,\ldots,a_k\) such that
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{x}=\sum_{i=1}^k a_i\mathbf{s}_i. \end{equation*}
    By linearity,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{y}=T(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{i=1}^k a_iT(\mathbf{s}_i). \end{equation*}
    Each \(T(\mathbf{s}_i)\) lies in \(T(S)\text{,}\) so every vector in \(\operatorname{Im}(T)\) is a linear combination of vectors in \(T(S)\text{.}\) Thus \(T(S)\) spans \(\operatorname{Im}(T)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 1.8.

    Let \(S=\{\mathbf{x}_1,\ldots,\mathbf{x}_n\}\) be a set of \(n\) vectors in a vector space \(V\text{.}\) Define \(T:\mathbb{R}^n\to V\) by
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} T(\mathbf{y})=\sum_{i=1}^n y_i\mathbf{x}_i. \end{equation*}
    Show that if \(\ker(T)\neq\{\mathbf{0}\}\text{,}\) then the vectors in \(S\) are linearly dependent.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Since \(\ker(T)\neq\{\mathbf{0}\}\text{,}\) there is a nonzero vector \(\mathbf{y}=(y_1,\ldots,y_n)\) such that \(T(\mathbf{y})=\mathbf{0}\text{.}\) Therefore
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} y_1\mathbf{x}_1+\cdots+y_n\mathbf{x}_n=\mathbf{0}. \end{equation*}
    Because \(\mathbf{y}\neq\mathbf{0}\text{,}\) at least one coefficient \(y_i\) is nonzero. This is a nontrivial linear relation among the vectors in \(S\text{,}\) so they are linearly dependent.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 1.9.

    Show that there is no linear transformation \(T:\mathbb{R}^2\to\mathbb{R}^3\) such that \(\operatorname{Im}(T)=\mathbb{R}^3\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Let \(\mathbf{e}_1,\mathbf{e}_2\) be the standard basis of \(\mathbb{R}^2\text{.}\) Every vector in the image has the form
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} T(a\mathbf{e}_1+b\mathbf{e}_2)=aT(\mathbf{e}_1)+bT(\mathbf{e}_2). \end{equation*}
    Thus \(\operatorname{Im}(T)\) is spanned by at most two vectors, so \(\dim(\operatorname{Im}(T))\leq 2\text{.}\) But \(\dim(\mathbb{R}^3)=3\text{.}\) Therefore the image cannot equal \(\mathbb{R}^3\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Subsection 1.4 ... And its Applications

    Checkpoint 1.10.

    In Dungeons and Dragons, you roll a twenty-sided die to make decisions. If you roll a \(20\text{,}\) you get a critical hit, and if you roll a \(1\text{,}\) you get a critical failure. Assuming the die is fair, how many rolls do you expect to make until you get your first critical hit or critical failure?
    🔗
    Solution.
    On each roll, the probability of stopping is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} p=\frac{2}{20}=\frac{1}{10}. \end{equation*}
    The waiting time is geometric with success probability \(p\text{,}\) so its expectation is \(1/p=10\text{.}\) Equivalently, if \(E\) denotes the expected number of rolls, then
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} E=1+\frac{9}{10}E, \end{equation*}
    which gives \(E=10\text{.}\) Thus the expected number of rolls is \(10\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)
    Write down a collection of weighted graphs such that every nonnegative circulation of \(G\) is a nonnegative linear combination of them.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Order the edges as
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} ((1,2),(1,3),(2,3),(4,3),(3,1)). \end{equation*}
    Let
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{c}_1=(1,0,1,0,1),\qquad \mathbf{c}_2=(0,1,0,0,1). \end{equation*}
    The vector \(\mathbf{c}_1\) is the unit circulation around \(1\to2\to3\to1\text{,}\) and \(\mathbf{c}_2\) is the unit circulation around \(1\to3\to1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    To see that these generate all nonnegative circulations, write a circulation as \(\mathbf{w}=(a,b,c,d,e)\) in the same edge order. Flow conservation at vertices \(1,2,3,4\) gives
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} e=a+b,\qquad c=a,\qquad e=b+c+d,\qquad d=0. \end{equation*}
    Hence \(c=a\text{,}\) \(d=0\text{,}\) and \(e=a+b\text{.}\) For a nonnegative circulation, \(a,b\geq0\text{,}\) and therefore
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{w}=a\mathbf{c}_1+b\mathbf{c}_2. \end{equation*}

    Checkpoint 1.13.

    Let \(G=(N,E)\) be a directed graph with edge-weight function \(w:E\to\mathbb{R}\text{.}\) Show that the sum of the total inflow over all vertices equals the sum of the total outflow over all vertices.
    🔗
    Solution.
    For a vertex \(v\text{,}\) define
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \operatorname{in}(v)=\sum_{(u,v)\in E} w(u,v),\qquad \operatorname{out}(v)=\sum_{(v,u)\in E} w(v,u). \end{equation*}
    When we sum the inflows over all vertices, every directed edge \((u,v)\) contributes its weight exactly once, at its head \(v\text{.}\) Thus
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \sum_{v\in N}\operatorname{in}(v)=\sum_{(u,v)\in E}w(u,v). \end{equation*}
    Likewise, when we sum the outflows over all vertices, every directed edge contributes its weight exactly once, at its tail \(u\text{.}\) Therefore
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \sum_{v\in N}\operatorname{out}(v)=\sum_{(u,v)\in E}w(u,v). \end{equation*}
    The two totals are equal.
