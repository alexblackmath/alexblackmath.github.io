    Section 2 Subspaces and Dimension

    Let \(T: U \to V\) be a linear transformation. Then the image \(\text{Im}(T)\) is the range of \(T\text{.}\) That is, \(\text{Im}(T) = \{T(\vec{x}): \vec{x} \in U\}\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.1.

    Show that \(\text{Im}(T)\) is a linear subspace.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Note that \(T(\vec{0}) = \vec{0}\text{,}\) so \(\vec{0} \in \text{Im}(T)\text{.}\) Let \(\vec{x}, \vec{y} \in \text{Im}(T)\text{.}\) Then, by definition, there exist \(\vec{u}, \vec{v} \in U\) such that \(T(\vec{u}) = \vec{x}\) and \(T(\vec{v}) = \vec{y}\text{.}\) Then
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \vec{x} + \vec{y} = T(\vec{u}) + T(\vec{v}) = T(\vec{u}+\vec{v}) \in \text{Im}(T). \end{equation*}
    Similarly, let \(\lambda \in \mathbb{R}\text{.}\) Then
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \lambda \vec{x} = \lambda T(\vec{u}) = T(\lambda \vec{u}) \in \text{Im}(T). \end{equation*}
    Hence, \(\text{Im}(T)\) is closed under scaling.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Images and kernels of linear transformations from finite dimensional vector spaces are always finite dimensional.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.2.

    Let \(U\) be a finite dimensional vector space, let \(T: U \to V\) be a linear transformation, and let \(W\) be a linear subspace of \(U\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (a) Then \(W\) is finite dimensional with dimension at most dimension of \(U\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (b) Then \(\text{ker}(T)\) is finite dimensional with dimension at most dimension of \(U\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (c) Then \(\text{Im}(T)\) is finite dimensional.
    🔗
    Solution.
    For (a), note that a basis of \(W\) must also be linearly independent in \(U\text{.}\) Why does that mean it must have size at most the size of a basis of \(U\text{?}\)
    🔗
    There were some assumptions I made here that I realized actually were not as clear as I had hoped. Let \(B\) be a basis of \(W\text{.}\) Then, if \(B\) is a basis of \(U\text{,}\) they must have the same size and then we are done. Suppose otherwise then. Since \(B\) is linearly independent in \(W\text{,}\) it is linearly independent in \(U\text{.}\) Fix a basis \(C\) of \(U\text{.}\) Since \(B\) does not span \(U\) \(\text{span}(B) \subsetneq \text{span}(C)\text{.}\) Therefore, there exists \(\vec{w} \in C\) such that \(\vec{w} \notin \text{span}(B)\text{,}\) so \(B \cup \{\vec{w}\}\) is still independent. If \(B \cup \{w\}\) is not a basis, we can apply the same argument iteratively, and show that \(B \cup S\) is a basis for some choice of subset \(S\text{.}\) To make this formal, there exists a subset \(D \subseteq C\) of maximal size such that \(B \cup D\) is independent. Suppose for the sake of contradiction that \(B \cup D\) does not span \(U\text{,}\) there must be some \(\vec{x} \in C \setminus \text{span}(B \cup D)\text{.}\) Then \(D \cup \{\vec{x}\}\) is bigger than \(D\) with the same property, contradicting the maximality of \(D\text{.}\) Thus, \(B \cup D\) must be spanning and independent and therefore a basis of \(U\text{.}\) Hence, \(B\) is contained in a basis of \(U\) and is therefore smaller.
    🔗
    For (b), explain how this follows from part (a).
    🔗
    Note that \(\text{ker}(T)\) is a subspace of \(U\text{,}\) so it follows by (a).
    🔗
    For (c), take a basis \(\vec{v}_{1}, \dots, \vec{v}_{n}\) of \(U\text{.}\) Show that \(T(\vec{v}_{1}), \dots, T(\vec{v}_{n})\) spans \(\text{Im}(T)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Let \(\vec{x} \in \text{Im}(T)\text{.}\) Then \(\vec{x} = T(\vec{y})\) for some \(\vec{y} \in U\text{.}\) Therefore, since \(\vec{v}_{1}, \dots, \vec{v}_{n}\) are a basis,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \vec{y} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} a_{i} \vec{v}_{i}. \end{equation*}
    Then
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \vec{x} = T(\vec{y}) = T\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n} a_{i}\vec{v}_{i} \right) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} a_{i} T(\vec{v}_{i}) \in \text{span}(\{T(\vec{v}_{i}):1 \leq i \leq n\}). \end{equation*}
    Therefore,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \text{Im}(T) \subseteq \text{span}(T(\vec{v}_{1}), \dots, T(\vec{v}_{n})). \end{equation*}
    Since \(T(\vec{v}_{1}), \dots, T(\vec{v}_{n}) \in \text{Im}(T)\) and it is a subspace by the previous exercise,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \text{span}(T(\vec{v}_{1}), \dots, T(\vec{v}_{n})) \subseteq \text{Im}(T). \end{equation*}
    Therefore,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \text{span}(T(\vec{v}_{1}), \dots, T(\vec{v}_{n})) = \text{Im}(T). \end{equation*}
    It follows that \(T(\vec{v}_{1}), \dots, T(\vec{v}_{n})\) span \(\text{Im}(T)\text{,}\) which means they contain a basis and thus a basis has size at most \(n\) as desired.
    🔗

    Section 3 Rank-Nullity Theorem

    We define \(\text{rank}(T)\) to be the dimension of \(\text{Im}(T)\text{.}\) We define \(\text{nullity}(T)\) to be the dimension of the \(\text{ker}(T)\text{,}\) also called the null space of \(T\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Theorem 3.1.

    For any linear transformation \(T: U \to V\text{,}\) where \(U\) is finite dimensional, \(\text{rank}(T) + \text{nullity}(T) = \text{dim}(U)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    Note that, by the previous lemma, both \(\text{ker}(T)\) and \(\text{Im}(T)\) are finite dimensional. Take a basis \(\vec{u}_{1}, \dots, \vec{u}_{m}\) for \(\text{ker}(T)\) and a basis \(\vec{v}_{1}, \dots, \vec{v}_{k}\) for \(\text{Im}(T)\text{.}\) Then, by definition of the image, there exists \(\vec{w}_{1}, \dots, \vec{w}_{k} \in U\) such that \(T(\vec{w}_{i}) = \vec{v}_{i}\) for all \(1 \leq i \leq k\text{.}\)
    🔗
    I claim that \(\vec{u}_{1}, \dots, \vec{u}_{m}, \vec{w}_{1},\dots, \vec{w}_{k}\) is a basis for \(U\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Note that, by construction, \(m\) is dimension of the kernel which is equal to the nullity by definition. Similarly, \(k\) is the dimension of the image, which is equal to the rank by definition. If this is a basis, then \(U\) has a basis of size \(k + m\text{,}\) which is exactly the rank plus nullity meaning that \(\text{dim}(U) = \text{rank}(T) + \text{nullity}(T)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Thus, in what remains, all we need to do is prove this is a basis.
    🔗
    Let’s do spanning first. Let \(\vec{x} \in U\text{.}\) We want to show that
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \vec{x} \in \text{span}(\vec{u}_{1}, \dots, \vec{u}_{m}, \vec{w}_{1}, \dots, \vec{w}_{k}). \end{equation*}
    Then \(T(\vec{x}) \in \text{Im}(T)\text{.}\) Since \(\vec{v}_{1}, \dots, \vec{v}_{k}\) is a basis for \(\text{Im}(T)\text{,}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} T(\vec{x}) = \sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i} \vec{v}_{i} \end{equation*}
    for some \(a_{1}, \dots, a_{k} \in \mathbb{R}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Note that
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} T\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k} a_{i} \vec{w}_{i} \right) = \sum_{i=1}^{k} a_{i}T(\vec{w}_{i}) = \sum_{i=1}^{k} a_{i} \vec{v}_{i} = T(\vec{x}). \end{equation*}
    It follows that
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} T(\vec{x}) - T\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k} a_{i} \vec{w}_{i}\right) = T(\vec{x} - \sum_{i=1}^{k} a_{i}\vec{w}_{i}) = \vec{0}. \end{equation*}
    Thus, by definition,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \vec{x} - \sum_{i=1}^{k} a_{i}\vec{w}_{i} \in \text{ker}(T). \end{equation*}
    Then, since \(\vec{u}_{1}, \dots, \vec{u}_{m}\) are a basis for the kernel,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \vec{x} - \sum_{i=1}^{k} a_{i}\vec{w}_{i} \in \text{span}(\vec{u}_{1}, \dots, \vec{u}_{m}). \end{equation*}
    It follows that there exist \(b_{1}, \dots, b_{m} \in \mathbb{R}\) such that
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \vec{x} - \sum_{i=1}^{k} a_{i}\vec{w}_{i} = \sum_{j=1}^{m} b_{j} \vec{u}_{j}. \end{equation*}
    By isolating \(\vec{x}\text{,}\) we find
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \vec{x} = \sum_{i=1}^{k} a_{i}\vec{w}_{i} +\sum_{j=1}^{m} b_{j} \vec{u}_{j}\in \text{span}(\vec{u}_{1}, \dots, \vec{u}_{m}, \vec{w}_{1}, \dots, \vec{w}_{k}). \end{equation*}
    Hence, it is spanning.
    🔗
    Next we need to show independence. Suppose that
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \sum_{i=1}^{k} a_{i}\vec{w}_{i} + \sum_{j=1}^{m} b_{j} \vec{u}_{j} = \vec{0}. \end{equation*}
    We want to show that the \(a_{i}\) and \(b_{j}\) are all then equal to \(0\text{.}\) The key idea is to rewrite it like this:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \sum_{i=1}^{k} a_{i}\vec{w}_{i} = -\sum_{j=1}^{m} b_{j} \vec{u}_{j}. \end{equation*}
    Since \(\vec{u}_{1}, \dots, \vec{u}_{m} \in \ker(T)\) and \(\ker(T)\) is a subspace,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \sum_{i=1}^{k} a_{i}\vec{w}_{i} = -\sum_{j=1}^{m} b_{j} \vec{u}_{j} \in \ker(T). \end{equation*}
    Thus,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \sum_{i=1}^{k} a_{i} \vec{v}_{i} = \sum_{i=1}^{k} a_{i}T(\vec{w}_{i}) = T\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k} a_{i} \vec{w}_{i} \right) = \vec{0}. \end{equation*}
    Then, since \(\vec{v}_{1},\dots, \vec{v}_{k}\) are a basis, they are in particular independent meaning that all of the \(a_{i}\) must be equal to \(0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Thus,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} -\sum_{j=1}^{m} b_{j} \vec{u}_{j} = \vec{0}. \end{equation*}
    Since \(\vec{u}_{1},\dots, \vec{u}_{m}\) are a basis for \(\text{ker}(T)\) they are independent meaning that all \(b_{j}\) must also equal \(0\text{.}\) Hence, by definition \(\vec{u}_{1}, \dots, \vec{u}_{m}, \vec{w}_{1}, \dots, \vec{w}_{k}\) are linearly independent. They are also spanning and thus a basis, which completes the proof.

    Section 4 Exercises

    Given a linear transformation \(T: \mathbb{R}^{n} \to \mathbb{R}^{m}\text{.}\) We can define \(\vec{a}_{i} = T(e_{i})\text{,}\) where \(e_{1}, \dots, e_{n}\) is the standard basis of \(\mathbb{R}^{n}\text{.}\) Then the matrix of \(T\) is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} A =\begin{pmatrix} \vec{a}_{1} & \vec{a}_{2} & \dots & \vec{a}_{n}\end{pmatrix}. \end{equation*}

    Checkpoint 4.1.

    Let \(T : \mathbb{R}^{n} \to \mathbb{R}^{m}\) be a linear transformation, and let \(A\) be its matrix. Show that
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} T(\vec{x}) = A \vec{x}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    What this illustrates is that a linear transformation is determined entirely by its behavior on a basis. In general, we have:
    🔗

    Checkpoint 4.2.

    Let \(T_{1}, T_{2}: U \to V\) be linear transformations. Let \(B\) be a basis for \(U\text{.}\) Suppose that \(T_{1}(\vec{x}) = T_{2}(\vec{x})\) for all \(\vec{x} \in B\text{.}\) Then \(T_{1} = T_{2}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    There is a reverse direction to this. You can define an arbitrary function \(f: B \to V\text{.}\) Then there is always a unique linear transformation that agrees with that function.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 4.3.

    Let \(U\) and \(V\) be vector spaces, and let \(B \subseteq U\) be a basis. Let \(f:B \to V\text{.}\) Then define \(T: U \to V\) by
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} T(\vec{x}) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} a_{i} f(\vec{v}_{i}), \end{equation*}
    whenever \(\vec{x} = \sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i} \vec{v}_{i}\) for \(\vec{v}_{1}, \dots, \vec{v}_{n} \in B\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (a) Prove that for any \(\vec{u} \in B\text{,}\) \(T(\vec{u}) = f(\vec{u})\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (b) Prove that \(T\) is well-defined. That is each input has a unique output.
    🔗
    (c) Prove that \(T\) respects scalar multiplication.
    🔗
    (d) Prove that \(T\) respects addition.
